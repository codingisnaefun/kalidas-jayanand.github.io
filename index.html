Case 01 Adopted NHS Lothian · 2023–2025

Why people stopped coming to the OST clinic

Opioid substitution therapy clinic, The Access Place — NHS primary care for people experiencing homelessness, Edinburgh Health & Social Care Partnership

Context

The Access Place is a primary care service for people experiencing homelessness in Edinburgh. Its opioid substitution therapy clinic dispenses scheduled medication on set days to a caseload of around a hundred patients.

A colleague and I noticed missed appointments at the OST clinic climbing. That costs more here than a missed appointment usually does. The service isn’t licensed to hold scheduled medication in-house overnight, so every dose that isn’t collected has to go back to pharmacy and be reissued — a controlled-drugs return, staff time on both sides, and a patient who goes without.

The missed appointments were being counted. Nobody had asked the patients why.

Method

We set a threshold and worked to it: every patient who missed two or more consecutive OST appointments was someone we spoke to. Not a convenience sample of whoever happened to be easy to catch — a defined group, identified from the records, approached in the clinic.

Two consecutive misses is the point where a pattern separates from a bad week, and it’s also the point where the clinical risk starts to matter. Using it as the inclusion rule meant we were systematically talking to the people the service was in the process of losing, rather than the ones still turning up.

Beyond that it was qualitative work with no budget and no formal protocol: two staff on a front desk deciding the reason behind a number was worth more than the number. Working the desk was the access. These are patients who don’t fill in feedback forms and don’t complain through channels, and the only reason they talked to us is that they already knew us.

Patient interviews Threshold-based sampling DNA record review Front-line observation Thematic grouping Vision (clinical records)
Finding

Not one reason we heard was about not wanting treatment. Every one was about the clinic being hard to attend.

  • Timing. Clinic hours didn’t fit around people’s days, and there was no flexibility in them.
  • No reminder. Nothing went out ahead of an appointment. People weren’t refusing to come; they were losing track of the day.
  • Dead phone numbers. Contact details on record were out of date, so the service couldn’t have reminded people even if it had tried.

Framed as patients who don’t attend, the failure sits inside the patient and there’s nothing to do about it. Framed as a clinic that’s awkward to reach and never reminds you, it sits inside the service — where somebody can actually fix it.

Decision
Adopted Clinicians in the OST clinic now call their own patients each morning before clinic opens, with a text reminder going out the following day.

We took the findings to senior management with that recommendation attached. It costs a few minutes of clinician time each morning and no money at all, which is a large part of why it was agreed rather than admired.

Impact
+30% rise in attendance at the OST clinic across the two months after the change

Fewer controlled-drugs returns, less pharmacy rework, and a hundred-odd people more likely to get the dose they were prescribed.

What I’d
do again
  • Treat a shared hunch as a research question. Two people noticing the same thing is a signal, but it isn’t evidence until somebody goes and checks.
  • Ask the people who stopped coming, and define “stopped” before you start. Two consecutive misses gave us a rule instead of a hunch about who to approach, and meant nobody who mattered got skipped because they were hard to catch.
  • Refuse the framing that locates the failure in the user. “Did not attend” describes a service at least as much as it describes a person.
  • Recommend something the service can actually afford. A perfect intervention nobody has capacity for is worth less than a phone call that happens every morning.
Case 02 In build WHO-funded cluster RCT · 2026

The app wasn’t ready to test on anyone yet

SHRADDHA-CMM — app-based lifestyle intervention for cardiometabolic multimorbidity, ~2,000 participants across Kerala

Context

SHRADDHA-CMM is a WHO-funded cluster randomised trial testing an app-based lifestyle intervention for cardiometabolic multimorbidity across roughly 2,000 participants in Kerala. The intervention group is largely older adults with limited smartphone experience, reached through community health workers rather than clinicians.

When I joined, the app was bones. The logic and the screens existed, but it had been built to satisfy the protocol rather than to be used by anybody in particular.

The plan was to take it to participants for usability testing. Before spending that access — and it is expensive access, in a rural population, through health workers with day jobs — somebody needed to check the build could survive contact with a real person.

Method

A structured expert review, run by me and the other research coordinator. We walked the app end to end against the conditions it would actually meet: an older adult in Kerala, on a cheap phone, with a health worker beside them and a queue behind that health worker.

Two reviewers rather than one was deliberate. Expert review is only as good as the reviewers’ blind spots, and two people flag different things — then you argue about the ones only one of you saw, which is usually where the interesting findings are.

We assessed three things in parallel: whether the build worked at all, whether it would be usable by this population, and whether it captured what the trial needed to monitor.

Structured expert review Functional defect audit Heuristic walkthrough Feature gap analysis Requirements writing
Finding

The app wasn’t in a state where usability testing would have told us anything. Participants would have hit broken buttons long before they hit a design decision.

  • Functional defects. Buttons that did nothing, placeholder content still sitting in live screens, and a broadcast messaging system glitching in ways that would have looked, to a participant, like the trial had gone quiet on them.
  • Usability for the actual population. Interface conventions that assume you have used an app before, on screens that assume you can read them.
  • Missing monitoring. No dashboards and no view counts on the IEC materials — so there was no way to tell whether anyone was engaging with the intervention content at all. In a trial, that isn’t a reporting inconvenience. If you can’t measure whether the intervention was received, you can’t interpret the result.

Running participant sessions on that build would have produced a long list of bug reports dressed up as user insight, and burned the access we needed for real testing later.

Output
Delivered to engineering A 20-point change list — defects, usability fixes and the monitoring features the trial needed — written as build requirements the software team could work through directly.

The format was deliberate. Findings that arrive as a narrative document get read, agreed with, and quietly deprioritised. A numbered list of specific changes gets worked through. I write for the person who has to act, which here was a developer with a backlog.

Impact
  • The build moved from protocol-shaped toward participant-shaped before the intervention group ever saw it.
  • Monitoring requirements went in as features rather than being retrofitted once the trial was running.
  • Participant usability testing is now scheduled against a build that can actually support it — which is the point of doing this first.
What I’d
do again
  • Check the build before spending user access. Participant time in a rural trial population is the scarcest resource in the project, and you get it once.
  • Review in pairs. The findings worth having are usually the ones only one of you spotted.
  • Ask what the trial needs to measure, not only what the user needs to do. Nobody requests a dashboard, and the study is unreadable without one.
  • Hand engineers a change list, not a report. Research that needs translating before anyone can act on it usually doesn’t get acted on.
Case 03 Replaced WHO-funded cluster RCT · 2026

The module that asked for the wrong thing

SHRADDHA-CMM — app-based lifestyle intervention for cardiometabolic multimorbidity, ~2,000 participants across Kerala

Context

Same trial as Case 02, different question. Once the app was usable, one planned arm still didn’t sit right: an alcohol-cessation module.

The behavioural model behind it was sound and the build was scoped. Nobody had asked whether participants would tell it the truth.

Method

Usability testing and contextual enquiry with the people who would actually be at either end of this module — community health workers collecting the data, and older adults in Kerala providing it. I also ran the training sessions for health workers on the questionnaire, which turned out to be the richest research setting available: watching where people hesitate while they think they're being taught is more honest than asking them afterwards.

Usability testing Contextual enquiry Observed training sessions Community health worker interviews Requirements writing
Finding

Alcohol self-reporting in this population is a stigma problem, not an interface problem. No amount of good design would produce honest data.

Participants would be disclosing to a community health worker who lives in their village and will still be there next year. That relationship is the reason the trial works at all — and it is exactly what makes candid alcohol disclosure unlikely. This wasn't a fixable usability defect. It sat underneath the product.

Which meant the module would have generated data, passed its own validation, and been wrong. The most expensive kind of failure: the kind that looks like success until analysis.

Decision
Replaced The alcohol-cessation module came out of the app and was replaced with printed IEC materials and helpline numbers for alcohol and smoking support — information participants can take away without disclosing anything to anyone.

I put the evidence to the Principal Investigator and the Associate Dean of Research. The argument wasn’t that the need had gone away — participants still needed alcohol and smoking support. It was that the app was the wrong channel to deliver it through, because the app required a disclosure the setting made unlikely. A leaflet and a phone number keep the support and ask nothing in return.

Recommending that a funded team stop building something is a harder conversation than recommending a fix. It goes down considerably better when you arrive with the replacement already worked out.

Impact
  • Engineering effort redirected before the module was built, not after a pilot exposed it.
  • A trial arm that would have produced confidently unreliable data never entered the protocol.
  • Participants kept access to alcohol and smoking support, through a route that requires no disclosure to anyone.
  • The team now asks “will they tell us the truth?” at scoping, alongside “can they use it?”
What I'd
do again
  • Research the conditions around a feature, not only the feature. The interface was fine. The social setting it would live in was the problem.
  • Say the unwelcome thing early, while it’s still cheap. The same finding six months later is a write-off instead of a decision.
  • Bring a replacement, not just an objection. “This won’t work” is a problem for someone else; “this won’t work, here’s what does” is a decision they can take in the room.
  • Use training and onboarding as research. People show you where a product fails while they're learning it, long before they'll tell you.

A bias
I'll admit

I over-weight the people who can't complain. Two years on an NHS front desk with users who had been let down by systems before will do that. It makes me good at finding silent failure and slower than I should be at trusting satisfaction scores.
